# The method

## Why composite the real UI instead of recording or generating it

A screen recording is flat: the camera can't move, nothing can leave the window, and every cursor wobble is in the
shot. Generated video invents an interface that isn't the product. The look of high-end software films is neither: it
is the real interface as textures on planes in a 3D scene, with a virtual camera, depth of field, soft light and long
easing curves. Because each piece is captured separately, a single control can lift out of the app, the rest can fall
back out of focus, and a panel can become the next shot. That separation is what makes the UI feel like an object.

## The pieces

```
<project>/
├── project.json            name, site, app URL, capture options
├── capture/                plan.mjs (what to grab for THIS film), lib.mjs (helpers)
├── scene/
│   ├── film.js             THE FILM: timing table, beats, copy, build(), update(t)
│   ├── engine.js           renderer, camera, post FX, motion blur, review tools, window.__film
│   ├── tl.js               tracks, ramps, easing
│   └── index.html          the page: stage + transport + timing drawer + caption editor
├── render/                 render.mjs (video/stills), sheet.mjs (contact sheet), holds.mjs (caption read-times)
├── tools/                  bake-timing.mjs, beat-lock.mjs
├── music/                  ONLY if add-music.mjs was run
└── assets/
    ├── captured/           images + manifest.json (regenerated by npm run capture)
    ├── review/             notes.json, timing.json, copy.json, fonts.json (written by the review tools)
    ├── music/              a soundtrack, if any
    └── refs/               anything the person sends as reference
```

Two servers run: the person's app (captures drive it) and the scene (`npm run scene`, port 5174).

## The film is a pure function of time

`update(t, at)` sets every object from `t` alone. Nothing accumulates between frames. That gives you:

- frame-exact seeking (the scrubber, stills at any second, rendering any stretch);
- real motion blur: render 16 sub-frames across the shutter and average them in linear light;
- renders identical to what the person scrubbed.

Never use `requestAnimationFrame` deltas, physics steps or random numbers per frame in `film.js`. Seeded randomness
computed in `build()` is fine.

## The timing table is the edit

Every action time is a row in `TIMING` (`film.js`), with a plain label in `LABELS`:

- a number = seconds from the start;
- `{ after: 'key', by: 0.4 }` = rides along with another action (moves when it moves);
- names ending in `0`/`1` = start/end of one move (the drawer lights the row while it runs);
- names ending in `Dur` = lengths; `end` = the film's length.

`retime(at)` rebuilds tracks from the resolved times; captions name their rows (`on: ['k0', 'k1']`), so nothing is
re-timed by hand. The person edits rows in the Timing drawer; edits save to `assets/review/timing.json` and every render
uses them. When a timing pass is agreed, `node tools/bake-timing.mjs` writes it into `film.js`.

## The loop

1. The person scrubs the live scene and says what's wrong (usually with a screenshot or a note at a frame).
2. You change timing rows, motion, or capture a missing piece.
3. Render stills across the affected beat and look at them yourself.
4. Render a preview, send it, commit.

Keep each round small. Ten short rounds beat one long one.

## Storyboard shapes that tend to work for software

- **Open on the product's own object**, alone, with its name: a card, a document, a canvas, a row. Not a logo.
- **The one feature as a physical action.** Pieces fly in, connect, and snap into the result.
- **Pull back to "it's a full app"**: the chrome arrives around what you were looking at.
- **One deep beat** on a second feature, played as the real click sequence at hand speed, ending on a result the
  viewer can judge in one glance (a before/after, a number that changes, a list that sorts).
- **End card**: name and URL, three to four seconds of air.

Transitions: prefer "the thing you're looking at becomes the next shot" (a row opens into a panel, a card becomes the
canvas) over cuts. A cut is for a change of idea.

These are shapes, not a template. Their product decides the beats.
